problem:  
For each dimension \( n \), let \( \mathcal{M}_\varepsilon^n \) denote the class of closed, simply connected Riemannian \( n\)-manifolds satisfying  
\[
1 \le K_g \le 1+\varepsilon, \quad \mathrm{diam}(M,g) \le \pi,
\]
and denote by  
\[
\delta_{GH}(M,S^n) = d_{GH}\big((M,g), (S^n, g_{S})\big)
\]
the Gromov–Hausdorff distance from the round unit \( n\)-sphere.

**Question:**  
Determine the **asymptotically optimal rate**  
\[
\delta_{GH}(M, S^n) = O(\varepsilon^{\alpha(n)}),
\]
if it exists, where \( \alpha(n) \) is the smallest exponent for which such an estimate holds whenever \( M \in \mathcal{M}_\varepsilon^n \).  
Is it true that for each fixed \( n \), such a rate exists and that the exponent \( \alpha(n) \) is universal (independent of topology)?  
Moreover, are there non-spherical topologies for which the exponent saturates any such bound, or does near-constant curvature force \( M \) to be *quantitatively* diffeomorphic to \( S^n \)?  

Why is it a "good" problem:  
This formulation moves beyond classical rigidity to probe the *quantitative geometry* underlying sphere theorems. While it is known that \( (M,g) \to S^n \) in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense as \( \varepsilon \to 0 \) (from Toponogov and Colding-type stability), the precise *rate* of convergence—and whether it is universal or topology-dependent—remains unknown.  

The problem synthesizes several deep aspects of modern geometry:
- **Comparison geometry**, using curvature pinching and diameter normalization;
- **Quantitative stability theory**, extending Cheeger–Colding’s framework from Ricci to sectional curvature;
- **Metric geometry**, through explicit convergence rate estimates in the Gromov–Hausdorff topology.

Establishing optimal exponents would create a bridge between qualitative rigidity (classical sphere theorems) and quantitative geometry (metric stability with rates). It challenges current analytic tools to capture how fine curvature pinching quantitatively constrains the global geometry, making it a simple-to-state yet profoundly deep open problem.
