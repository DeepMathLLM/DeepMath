problem:  
Let \(M^n \subset S^{n+1}(1)\) be a compact, embedded **isoparametric minimal hypersurface** with \(g\) distinct principal curvatures.  
For each unit vector \(v \in S^{n+1}(1)\), let \(h_v(x) = \langle x , v \rangle\)\ be the height function on \(M\), and let  
\[
\mu_i(M,v) = \#\{\,p \in M \mid \nabla h_v(p) = 0, \operatorname{ind}_p(h_v) = i\,\}
\]
be the number of its critical points with Morse index \(i\).  
Let  
\[
\mathbb{E}_v[\mu_i(M)] = \int_{S^{n+1}} \mu_i(M,v)\, d\sigma(v)
\]
denote the averaged count over unit directions with respect to the uniform measure.  

The symmetry group \(H_M \subset \mathrm{SO}(n+2)\) acts transitively on each curvature leaf of \(M\), with isotropy representation \(\rho_M: H_M \to \mathrm{O}(T_x M)\).  

**Conjectural problem:**  
For isoparametric minimal hypersurfaces in \(S^{n+1}(1)\), does there exist a universal relation
\[
\mathbb{E}_v[\mu_i(M)] = \Psi_i(g,\, \text{multiplicities of principal curvatures},\, \chi(M))
\]
that is independent of the particular homogeneous realization of \(M\)?  
Equivalently, do the integral-geometric Morse index statistics of random height functions depend only on the algebraic data of the isoparametric family (i.e., \(g\) and its curvature multiplicities), and not on the specific embedding or ambient symmetry subgroup?  

Why is it a "good" problem:  
This version isolates the conjecture to the class of **isoparametric minimal hypersurfaces**, where the geometry and symmetry are tightly interwoven yet not fully classified beyond specific multiplicity patterns. The problem is mathematically concrete—asking for an existence of a universal formula relating average Morse data to intrinsic curvature-algebraic invariants—and lies at the intersection of **Morse theory**, **integral geometry**, and **representation theory**.  
It also probes a possible bridge between abstract algebraic data that characterize isoparametric families (through Cartan’s equations and multiplicities) and analytic invariants arising from random Morse functions. A resolution would illuminate whether Morse-theoretic information can detect (or fail to detect) geometric homogeneity, potentially revealing new invariants that measure degrees of hidden symmetry.  
Its formulation is rigorous yet tightly focused, and it touches a core structure uniting geometry, topology, and symmetry—thus making it a genuinely “good” and fertile research-level problem.
